What these numbers can and cannot tell you

Every calculator here assumes something that is rarely true in the field: that your respondents are a random draw from the population you care about. They almost never are. Panel respondents are people who sign up for panels. Customer list surveys reach people who still open your email.

Sampling error is the small problem

The margin of error only measures the luck of the draw. It says nothing about a leading question, a broken screener, or a panel that skews young. Those biases do not shrink when you add respondents.

Subgroups eat your sample

A study of 400 sounds comfortable until you split it six ways and report on a cell of 38. Decide your reporting cuts before fieldwork, then size for the smallest cell you plan to quote.

Top-down sizing drifts

Multiplying a big population by a plausible-looking percentage produces a confident number with no evidence in it. Build the figure bottom-up from real pipeline where you can, and use the top-down number only as a sanity check.

Significance is not importance

With 20,000 respondents, a one-point gap becomes statistically significant. Ask what size of difference would change your decision, and check whether you found one that large.
